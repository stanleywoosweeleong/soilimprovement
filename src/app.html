About Dr. Huang

A soil doctor who reads the field before the fertilizer bag

Dr. Huang is an associate researcher in soil and fertilizer science at the Tainan District Agricultural Research and Extension Station (臺南區農業改良場), part of Taiwan's Ministry of Agriculture. Dr. Huang has worked in its soil and fertilizer lab since 2001 and has led a branch station. The research covers soil fertility, organic farming, soil microbes and biofertilizer development.

The central claim is simple: about 80% of the crop problems farmers bring to Dr. Huang trace back to the soil, not to pests. Most of those soil problems are caused by too much fertilizer, not too little.

Each year the station issues about 8,000 soil-test reports with a fertilizer prescription for each crop stage, and Dr. Huang personally signs about 2,000 of them.

Dr. Huang's published output spans more than two decades: at least 16 papers in the station's Research Bulletin, 9 technical handbooks, and many extension articles. The topics run from composting food waste and vermicompost, through mycorrhizal fungi and liquid organic fertilizer, to cadmium-safe crop choices and soil steam sterilization.

The thinking behind the methods

Thirteen principles from Dr. Huang's work

These come from Dr. Huang's own articles and interviews. Each one explains why a method works, so it can be adapted to Malaysian soils and crops.

Principle 5, illustrated

Why rain-shelter and greenhouse soils turn salty

The roof blocks rain that would wash leftover fertilizer down. Heat raises evaporation, and water moving up through the soil carries dissolved salts to the surface. When growth slows, farmers often add more fertilizer, which makes it worse. Fix: test EC, flood-leach, cut fertilizer.

Case study · organic farm in Tainan · reported 2020

From a hardy melon to a delicate premium variety, organically

Fa Fu Tao Organic Farm (發福桃有機農場) worked with Dr. Huang for about four years on organic melon and mango. What they did:

  • Followed Dr. Huang's custom fertilizer plan, built from a free soil test, the crop's needs at each stage, and the organic fertilizer brands the farm could buy.
  • Inoculated seedlings with mycorrhizal fungi.
  • Adjusted feeding to the weather during the season.
  • Widened row and plant spacing for airflow, and avoided leaf feeding, which raises pest numbers.
  • Grew natural grass cover in the mango orchard to build soil life.

Result: the farm moved from the hardier 'Tainan No. 12' melon to the demanding 'Arus' variety, with thick, upright leaves, and grew sweet 'Summer Snow' mangoes. Compost on the farm was made from goat manure and grain husks.

Your soil report

Quick field EC test Dr. Huang uses: 15 g soil + 75 mL water (a 1:5 ratio), shake, and read with a handheld EC meter. Up to 0.6 dS/m is normal. Test topsoil (0–20 cm) and subsoil (20–40 cm) separately. If the topsoil is saltier, fertilizer is piling up at the surface.

    Symptom guide

    Pick where on the plant you see the problem. Each card gives the symptom, the usual causes and Dr. Huang's fixes with doses. The rule: confirm the symptom, find the cause, then treat. Many "deficiencies" are really an excess of another nutrient, so check a soil test before adding more fertilizer.

    Rates in the handbooks are per hectare; they are shown here per acre (1 acre = 0.405 ha). Spray strengths are given as a percentage of product in water (0.5% = 5 g per litre) or as a dilution (500× = 2 mL or 2 g per litre). For trace elements the general rule is a leaf spray at 500–1,000× dilution, once or twice a week until the symptom clears.

    Step by step

    Soil recovery playbook

    Dr. Huang's methods, put in working order for a degraded plot. Steps 1–5 repair the soil. Steps 6–9 keep it healthy. Retest after 6–12 months.

    育苗期拌菌根、田间灌溶磷、肥料减施三分之一 Dr. Huang's 2026 summary formula: mix mycorrhizal fungi into the nursery mix, drench phosphate-solubilizing bacteria in the field, and cut chemical fertilizer by one-third.

    1. Diagnose before you fertilize 因土施肥

      • Test pH, EC, organic matter, P, K, Ca, Mg and trace elements. Sample 0–20 cm and 20–40 cm separately.
      • Check irrigation water EC and pH too, especially well water.
      • Problem across a whole area: suspect soil or fertilizer. Problem on single plants: suspect pests, disease or weak seedlings.
      • Collect your history: fertilizer type, amount, frequency and crop sequence.
      • Then follow Dr. Huang's three steps: the soil test sets the starting fertilizer rate, field diagnosis of the crop (leaf analysis and the Symptom guide) adjusts it, and better methods raise efficiency.

      Why: deficiency symptoms are often caused by an excess of another nutrient (antagonism). Adding the "missing" nutrient makes it worse.

    2. Break the hardpan and fix drainage

      • Water that takes 2–3 weeks to drain points to a compacted plough pan, usually 20–40 cm deep.
      • Deep plough, or swap topsoil and subsoil, to break it. This also dilutes surface salt.
      • Subsoil is poorer and less porous, so add organic matter afterwards (step 5).
      • Till only at the right moisture. Working wet soil makes large clods that hurt growth.

    3. Flush out excess salt (if EC > 0.6)

      • First test irrigation water EC. If it is salty, use deeper wells (over 120 m in coastal Taiwan) or another low-EC source.
      • Till or stir the soil, then flood so water stands above the surface.
      • Hold the flood for about one week, then drain.
      • Retest EC. Repeat until it is below 0.6 dS/m.
      • Well-drained soil does not need stirring. Simply flood and drain repeatedly until EC is normal.
      • If water still won't drain, install subsurface drains: gravel-filled trenches with perforated pipe. Where there is no natural outlet, bury a 500-litre collection drum in a corner of the greenhouse and pump it out.
      • Other options: bring in clean soil, replace the topsoil, or grow salt-absorbing crops (maize, sesbania 田菁) and plough them in.

      Result in Budai: EC 3.41 → 0.33 dS/m with deep ploughing plus flooding.

    4. Correct pH toward 5.5–6.8 (ideally about 6.5)

      Dr. Huang's 2018 article: correcting pH is the most effective and fastest way to improve a soil's nutrient supply, because near 6.5 almost every nutrient is at its most available.

      • Too acidic (common in Malaysia): apply ground magnesium limestone (GML) or dolomite based on a lime-requirement test. Low pH locks phosphorus onto iron and aluminium.
      • Too alkaline: use acidifying materials. In the Tainan peanut program, sulfur powder plus organic fertilizer was used.
      • Avoid years of heavy high-pH organic fertilizer or lime. It raises pH, and ammonium fertilizer then escapes as ammonia gas that can scorch plants.

    5. Rebuild organic matter with coarse fibre

      • To raise organic matter, choose low-nutrient, high-fibre materials: bagasse, cattle manure, spent mushroom substrate, rice husk or straw compost.
      • Rate: 4–8 tonnes per acre, mixed thoroughly into the soil.
      • Timing: broadcast organic and chemical fertilizer about 10 days before planting, then plough them in.
      • Count the nutrients in the organic fertilizer and subtract them from the chemical fertilizer. Include leftovers from earlier applications.
      • Don't rely only on pelleted or oil-seed meal (粕类) fertilizers. They are rich and convenient but decompose fast in hot, humid weather, and organic matter keeps falling. Always add coarse-fibre material too.
      • Use certified, mature compost. Poor commercial organic fertilizer can leave a salt layer and stop seeds germinating.
      • Coarse organic matter plus microbes is how Dr. Huang builds disease-suppressive soil (抑病土).

    6. Reintroduce beneficial microbes

      Inoculate at the nursery stage. It is the cheapest point and gives the highest success rate.

      Mycorrhizal fungi (AMF)1–2 g spore-soil per cell (~100 spores/g)
      Mix into peat:vermiculite or perlite (1:1 by volume), fill trays, then sow. Target 50–100 spores per seedling.
      Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB)~4×10⁸ cfu/mL, dilute 300–500×
      Drench the root zone until wet so the liquid reaches the roots.
      Rhizobium (legumes)dilute 5–10×, ≥10⁶ cfu/mL
      Soak or spray seed, air-dry, then sow.
      N-fixer + Bacilluspeanut program, 2023–24
      Adding Bacillus B40 to an N-fixer raised peanut yield 15% in year one and 65% in year two.
      • Nursery media for AMF: keep nitrogen under 250 ppm and phosphorus under 30 ppm. AMF seedlings may look smaller and paler at first, which is normal.
      • AMF does not colonize cruciferous leafy greens (cabbage family) or amaranth.

    7. Cut chemical fertilizer and apply the "five rights"

      • Right site, right crop, right time, right amount, right method.
      • Keep soil moist when feeding. Phosphorus and potassium only reach roots through soil water, so fertilizer is wasted on dry soil. Drip irrigation helps both go further.
      • For deep-rooted crops such as corn, place potassium deeper rather than on the surface.
      • Adjust the plan to the weather as the season goes. For example, raise the potassium share when it is very hot.
      • On slopes, split fertilizer into more, smaller applications so rain doesn't wash it away.
      • Don't overfeed seedlings. Heavily fed seedlings become poor nutrient absorbers after transplanting.
      • Don't pile on potassium for sweetness. It causes magnesium and boron shortages that lower fruit quality. Heavy late top-dressing also lowers melon sugar.
      • With microbes, use about ⅔ of the usual rate. In melon trials this gave 13.9% more yield than the full rate.
      • Split doses by growth stage. Avoid fixed-formula compound fertilizer that ignores what is already in the soil.
      • Cut base fertilizer according to soil EC (the melon handbook rule, EC 1:5):
        Soil EC (dS/m)Base fertilizer
        above 0.4None
        0.3 – 0.4Cut usual amount by ¾
        0.2 – 0.3Cut usual amount by ¼
        0.1 – 0.2Usual amount
        below 0.1Full recommended rate
      • Melon split (% of each nutrient): N 40 / 40 / 20, P 50 / 20 / 30, K 30 / 20 / 50. The three doses are base (before planting), first top-dress (5–7 days after pollination, after fruit thinning) and second top-dress (21–28 days after pollination, when netting completes).

    8. Rotate and use green manure

      • In greenhouses, rotate with flooded paddy rice. This leaches salt, reduces nematodes, and the rice or green manure adds organic matter when ploughed in.
      • Green manure suppresses weeds, improves fertility and protects soil from erosion.
      • Measured result of a rice rotation in a greenhouse: EC fell from 0.63 to 0.33 dS/m, and nematodes from 204 to 31 per 100 g soil (limit: under 40).
      • Chop crop residues and wet them before ploughing in, so they break down faster.
      • Plough melon fields within 1–2 days after harvest to cut Monosporascus root-rot spores.
      • Before planting, shrimp or crab shell powder (rich in chitin) lowers root-knot nematode egg numbers.
      • Avoid planting the same crop family repeatedly. Continuous melon cropping built up root-knot nematodes and Monosporascus root rot.

    9. Monitor and keep records

      • Keep a cultivation diary: planting dates, fertilizer type and amount.
      • Photograph the crop regularly.
      • Use leaf analysis alongside soil tests. It shows what the plant is actually absorbing and what it lacks.
      • Retest EC and pH every crop cycle in greenhouses, and yearly in open fields.
    Cautions from Dr. Huang's papers

    Things that undo the microbes

    • High-dose fast-release phosphate at nursery or transplant suppresses mycorrhizal colonization.
    • Soil-drench fungicides that harm AMF include benomyl, thiabendazole, thiophanate-methyl (Topsin), tridemorph (Calixin), triforine and triadimefon. Fosetyl-Al and metalaxyl (Ridomil) were reported to be compatible or even helpful.
    • Unregistered products: buy registered microbial fertilizers with stated live counts from reputable makers.
    • The "second seed" rule: every practice should let both the crop seed and the microbe spore thrive.
    Adapting to Malaysia

    Notes for tropical, high-rainfall farms

    • Open-field soils are mostly acidic and leached (often pH 4.5–5.5). Liming and organic matter usually come first, not salt leaching.
    • Rain shelters and greenhouses (e.g. Cameron Highlands) behave like the Taiwan greenhouses in these studies. Test EC there.
    • For acidic soils, ask suppliers which AMF genus they sell. Acaulospora and Entrophospora favour acidic soils. Most commercial Glomus prefer neutral to slightly acidic.
    • For heavy rain, use raised beds and good drainage. AMF-treated melons held more dry weight and better photosynthesis under flooding.

    These adaptation notes combine Dr. Huang's findings with general tropical soil practice. They are not taken from the papers.

    Homemade fertilizer · from Dr. Huang's 2014 handbook (2018 reprint)

    Fermented organic liquid fertilizer

    Recipes and rules from Making and Using Organic Liquid Fertilizer (有機液肥製作與應用, Huang & Chiang). Liquid fertilizer acts faster than compost because the nutrients are already dissolved. It feeds the crop quickly between slower solid organic applications. This is the course's "homemade microbes and ferments" topic (自制微生物/酵素).

    Type 1

    Leachate

    Liquid drained from a compost heap while it is still composting. Unstable content, so least useful.

    Type 2

    Extract

    Mature compost soaked in water. Stable nutrients and few pathogens, so more useful.

    Type 3 · the recipes below

    Fermented

    Organic materials mixed with water in set ratios and fermented. The mix can be adjusted to the crop's needs.

    At a glance

    Brewing in four steps

    Recipe calculator

    Ingredients for your drum

    IngredientHigh-NHigh-PHigh-K

    High-N: early and middle growth, and as an after-harvest feed for fruit trees. High-P and High-K: flowering and fruiting. For melons and other gourds after flowering, mix High-N and High-P/K in equal parts for balanced feeding.

    Swaps from the handbook: soybean meal can be replaced by rapeseed or peanut meal, or expired milk or eggs. Wood ash can be replaced by palm ash or crude potash. Palm ash is about 30% potash and is easy to find in Malaysia. The handbook does not name a microbe strain for the "beneficial microbes" (有益微生物); a registered effective-microbe or Bacillus product is the usual choice.

    How the recipes work

    Why these ingredients

    Every brew uses the same six roles. To push a brew toward nitrogen, phosphorus or potassium, raise the ingredient rich in that nutrient and cut the others. The handbook: the nutrient level and balance "can be adjusted by changing the proportions of the materials" (依不同有機資材之比例調整).

    "High" means high compared with the other two recipes. The High-P and High-K brews are clearly led by their nutrient. The High-N brew is closer to balanced: it has about twice the nitrogen of the others, but not more nitrogen than potassium. These are estimates of what goes into the drum, worked out from the handbook's ingredient values, assuming steamed bone meal and wood ash at about 23% K₂O.

    The finished liquid is weak but fast. The handbook's lab test of a High-N brew found only 0.15% N, 0.07% P₂O₅ and 0.11% K₂O, against 1.77% N in solid compost. Much of each ingredient stays in the sludge or is lost. Its EC was about 25 dS/m undiluted, which is why it must be diluted. Use it as a quick top-up between compost applications, not a replacement. The recipes come from a 2006 Harvest Farm Magazine article by Chien Hsuan-yu (簡宣裕) and colleagues, which the handbook cites.

      potassium source      		Potassium falls sharply. A High-K brew is no longer possible. The brew also stays more acidic, because ash is alkaline.*Palm ash (about 30% K₂O), crude potash, or seaweed powder (only 4% K₂O, so much more is needed).
      Sugar or molasses
      energy      		Microbes lose their quick energy. The handbook says brewing time depends on the carbon and energy supply, so it will be slower and more likely to go putrid.* Too much sugar is also a problem: an over-concentrated mix stops the microbes and starts to smell.Brown sugar or molasses, kept near 10–12 kg per 100 L.
      Beneficial microbes
      starter      		Fermentation relies on wild microbes from the materials and the air. The handbook warns that brews made without aeration or without added beneficial microbes are the most likely to turn foul.A registered effective-microbe or Bacillus product. Stir and aerate twice a day either way.
      Stirring or aeration
      process      		Without air the brew turns foul (ammonia, sulfide smells) and loses nitrogen as gas.Stir 3–5 minutes morning and evening, or use an aquarium pump.*

      Leaving an ingredient out does not make the brew dangerous. It changes its strength and balance. Whatever you use, check that it smells mildly sour, passes the seed test (80% germination or more), and is diluted before use. Points marked * are my interpretation, not stated in the handbook.

      Brewing

      Making it

      1. Put water and all ingredients in a plastic drum.
      2. Stir morning and evening for 3–5 minutes, or use a timed stirrer or aerator.
      3. Ferment for 10–20 days. Time depends on temperature, aeration, microbe numbers and the sugar supply.
      4. Measure EC to gauge strength. A higher EC needs more dilution.
      Signs it is ready
      • Fewer bubbles, and a dark brown to black colour.
      • A mild sour, lactic or soy-sauce smell. White fungal threads are normal.
      • Seed test: seeds watered with the diluted brew should germinate at 80% or more.
      • Undiluted pH between 4 and 9.
      Signs of a bad batch
      • A rotten smell or maggots mean the mix is too concentrated for the microbes, or it lacks air.
      • Fermenting longer than about 1.5 months uses up the sugar, and it starts to smell.
      Using it

      Dilution and timing

      MethodDilutionNote
      Soil drench30–50×No need to filter. Pump onto the root zone.
      Leaf spray100–200×Filter first. See caution below.
      • During long rainy, low-sun spells, or when too much nitrogen has hurt flowering, apply High-P/K every 4–5 days, at least twice.
      • Drench High-P/K in mid to late fruiting to raise sweetness.
      • For fruit trees, bury a pipe or open bucket in the root zone and pour the brew in. This feeds deeper soil.

      Use the finished brew within 2–3 months. Store it in a cool place out of direct sun.

      Eight cautions from the handbook

      Where liquid fertilizer goes wrong

      • Use only a mature brew. Leftover sugar feeds a burst of microbes in the soil that harms the crop.
      • Always dilute. Undiluted brew burns plants, especially sprayed on leaves.
      • Fix soil limits first. In alkaline soil, ammonium nitrogen escapes as gas. Acidic soil locks up phosphorus. Sandy soil loses nutrients quickly.
      • Keep it balanced. Don't rely on one brew, especially a high-nitrogen one.
      • Check the water. Avoid dirty wastewater. Let tap water stand in the sun first so the chlorine doesn't harm the microbes.
      • No high-N brew at flowering. It causes flower and fruit drop.
      • Store cool, use within 2–3 months.
      • Be careful spraying leaves. The sugars and acids feed microbes and pests on the leaf. A cherry tomato grower who sprayed it saw small insect numbers rise sharply.

      Long-term heavy use of high-EC liquid fertilizer or compost can itself salt the soil surface (melon handbook, 2016). Keep testing EC. The handbook says "eco-enzyme" (環保酵素) brews can also serve as liquid fertilizer, but only after checking their properties and nutrient content.

      Sourcing in Malaysia

      Where to get the microbes

      Dr. Huang's methods need three kinds of microbe: mycorrhizal fungi (AMF), phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB), and Bacillus or nitrogen-fixing bacteria. Choice in Malaysia is small, and I could not confirm that every product below is on sale today. Use this as a list of who to ask, not a shopping list.

      Mycorrhizal fungi (AMF)

      Seedling-mix powder

      Spores in sand or soil, mixed into the nursery mix at 1–2 g per cell. Local options exist, mostly sold for oil palm.

      Available locally
      Phosphate-solubilizing bacteria (PSB)

      Research bodies

      Advice and strains

      UPM · Nuclear Malaysia · Tainan station

      Not shops, but good contacts for local strains and advice.

      Details
      • Universiti Putra Malaysia: mycorrhiza research since the 1980s; its Bacillus strain UPMB10 supplied about 28% of young oil palms' nitrogen in trials.
      • Nuclear Malaysia: ran a biofertilizer program from 2002 to 2017 and sits on the working group for a Malaysian Standard for biofertilizers (since 2016).
      • Tainan station, Taiwan: Dr. Huang's own station makes AMF, PSB and Bacillus B40 products. Ask Dr. Huang at the course whether any can be supplied outside Taiwan.
      Before you buy

      Checklist

      • A stated live count. About 100 spores per gram for AMF, and about 10⁸ cfu per mL for PSB (the levels used in Dr. Huang's methods). No count means no way to judge it.
      • A reputable, registered maker. Dr. Huang's papers say to buy registered products with stated activity. Malaysia has no fertilizer law in force yet; the industry regulates itself, so ask for trial results.
      • Fresh and kept cool. Check the manufacture date. Microbes die in hot shops and car boots.
      • Suited to your soil. For acidic soil, ask which AMF genus it contains: Acaulospora and Entrophospora favour acid soil; most Glomus prefer neutral to slightly acidic.
      • Used correctly. No high-phosphate fertilizer at the nursery stage, and keep soil-drench fungicides such as benomyl and thiophanate-methyl away from AMF.
      Who to ask

      Good places to start

      • At the course (9 Dec 2026): ask Dr. Huang which strains and products would suit Malaysia. The course partners may also stock suitable products.
      • Your state Department of Agriculture (Jabatan Pertanian) office or MARDI extension staff: they know what is actually sold locally.
      • Online marketplaces: search "mikoriza", "baja bio" or "biofertilizer". Check the maker, live count and expiry before buying.
      Making your own

      What you can and can't make on the farm

      Possible

      Multiply AMF on the farm. Grow a host plant such as maize or sorghum in sterilized sand mixed with a little bought AMF. After 3–4 months, cut the tops, let the pots dry, and chop the roots into the sand. That mix is your own inoculum for the next nursery.*

      Not reliable

      Homemade PSB. You can't tell whether a homemade culture contains phosphate-dissolving strains. Homemade ferments (indigenous microorganisms, eco-enzyme, the liquid fertilizers) feed soil life in general, but they are not a substitute for a tested PSB product.
